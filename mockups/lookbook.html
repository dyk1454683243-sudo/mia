MIA Twelve plates · interface directions

Twelve ways the table could look.

The game already works: two to eight phones, six lives each, sixty seconds a turn, and a hardcoded ladder from 3·1 up to Mia. What follows is a lookbook, not a spec — some plates are an afternoon of CSS, some need state the server doesn't keep yet, and one or two are frankly silly. Every mockup below is drawn live in the page, so pinch and poke at them.

The standing read

  • keepsThe felt-and-brass palette is right. Dark green reads as a card table instantly, and the gold is doing real work marking whose turn it is.
  • keepsOne column, thumb-reachable. Everything important lands in the bottom third. Don't lose that.
  • thinThe announce grid is twenty-one identical buttons in reading order. The ranking is the whole game and the UI makes you already know it.
  • thinThe reveal is a paragraph. The most dramatic second in Mia currently arrives as body copy inside a card.
  • thinNo table. A vertical roster is a list of accounts; Mia is people sitting in a circle watching each other lie.

A list tells you who is playing. A circle tells you who is about to lie to you.

Seats sit on an ellipse, the cup's path is a brass needle pointing from the last announcer at you, and the standing claim lives dead centre where everyone's eyes already are. Eliminated players stay in their chair, greyed — Mia is a game you keep watching after you're out.

The speech bubble is the single biggest win: what someone claimed stays attached to their face, so "Damp Ferret has claimed a double three rounds running" becomes something you can see rather than something you have to remember.

Five seats fit comfortably; eight needs a tighter ellipse and initials-only avatars. Rotate the ring so you are always at the bottom, the way it works at a real table.

Build cost Client-only. Every field is already in the snapshot — players, lives, announcement, turn. It is a different renderPlaying(), nothing more.

Twenty-one buttons in a grid is a lookup table. One vertical ladder is the rules.

The ranking is hardcoded and famously counter-intuitive — 11 beats 65, 21 beats everything — so draw it as the ladder it is. The standing claim is a brass cut line: everything below it is dimmed and untappable, everything above it is a legal lie, and the rung you actually hold sits down in the dark where you can see how far you have to climb.

The list scrolls, one thumb, with the cut line pinned just under the fold so the cheapest legal claim is always the first thing under your finger. Little hints ride along the right: double, beats every mixed roll, costs 2 if they doubt and you're telling the truth.

A first-timer learns the ranking in one round instead of three games. That is the whole argument.

Build cost Client-only. legalMoves() and the RANKING array already give the exact ordering and the cut point.

🎲 or shake the phone to roll

Your dice are secret. Make secrecy something your hand does, not something a caption claims.

Right now the dice sit face-up in your own roster row for the whole round. It is correct — the server redacts everybody else's — but it doesn't feel secret, and it means anyone glancing over your shoulder on the sofa has the whole game.

So: the cup sits closed. Press and hold, the cup lifts and your two dice are there; lift your thumb and they're gone. It is the physical gesture from the real game, it solves the sofa problem, and it puts a tiny cost on looking — which is exactly the tension Mia runs on.

Then take the obvious next step: shake the phone to roll (devicemotion), with a rattle and a haptic tap when the dice settle. On desktop the cup is a click-and-hold, and the shake is the space bar.

Build cost Client-only, plus a permission prompt for motion on iOS. Worth gating behind a "tap to roll" fallback that always works.

This is the second everyone leans in for. Give it the whole screen and three beats.

Today the verdict arrives as a sentence inside a card. Instead, take over the screen for four seconds and play it: the cup lifts, the dice tumble and settle, the stamp slams down — BLUFF in red, TRUE in green, and MIA in brass with a double-weight thud when the real 21 costs the doubter two lives.

Note the asymmetry worth designing for: a caught bluff and an honest claim are opposite emotions and currently share a layout. Claimed-versus-actual side by side makes the comparison the picture, and the verdict line underneath just names it.

Auto-advance on a visible four-second timer so nobody is waiting on the one player who put their phone down — matching how the turn clock already fails safe.

Build cost Client-only for the staging; the reveal payload already carries claimed, actual, verdict and lives lost. Add a short "let it play" delay before the next round's alarm fires and you also need a server nudge.

The cheapest legal claim is 6·3 — that's nine rungs above what you're holding. There is no honest move left.

Reading the table

Damp Ferretbluffed 4 of 6
Loud Kettlebluffed 1 of 5
youdoubted 0 times

Glass table is on. Switch it off in table settings.

Mia is arithmetic about other people. Show the arithmetic; keep the people guessing.

The gap gauge. The distance between the dice in your cup and the cheapest thing you're allowed to say is your hand. Nine rungs means you are lying through your teeth and your face has to not show it. One rung means you can shrug and say 6·3 like it's nothing. The client can compute this today and currently doesn't say a word about it.

The clock as a ring that fills red rather than a grey "60S" pill — the same information, but it gets loud on its own in the last ten seconds instead of needing you to read it.

Reading the table is the speculative one: bluff rate per player, accumulated across the game. It makes late rounds much richer and it slightly ruins the bluffing, which is either the best or worst idea on this page. Ship it as a table option — Glass table: on — rather than a default.

Build cost Gauge and ring: client-only. Bluff rates need the room to keep per-player tallies across rounds and put them in the snapshot — real work in table-room.ts, and a decision about whether it belongs in the finished-game row in D1.

Six taps, no keyboard, nothing to moderate.

"There is no chat" is a good decision. "There is nothing to say" isn't.

Free text on a link-shareable table means moderation, abuse reports, and a whole class of problem this project has correctly refused. A fixed vocabulary of six emotes gets you most of the fun with none of that: nothing to moderate, nothing to translate, and a hard cap on how obnoxious anyone can be.

The trick is to let the emotes share the log rather than float over it, so the running story of the round and the peanut gallery read as one conversation. Rate-limit to one emote per player per turn and the noise problem solves itself.

The deliciously mean version: log who doubted whom as a running grudge, and open the post-game card with "Loud Kettle called you a liar four times, and was right twice."

Build cost Server work. A new client message kind, a broadcast, a per-turn rate limit, and a decision about whether emotes belong in the persisted log or are purely ephemeral. (Ephemeral is right — they should not survive a reconnect replay.)

07

Six lives, four ways

detail study

Pips — today

Compact, glanceable, already shipped. Weakness: six identical dots are hard to count at a glance and the difference between "four left" and "three left" is a dark dot at the end of a row.

Candles

Losing a life snuffs a flame and a thread of smoke drifts up. Reads instantly across the table, carries genuine dread at one flame left, and gives the whole board ambient motion. Costs vertical space.

Chalk tally

The pub original: lives scratched on a slate, struck through as they go. Countable at a glance in the way dots aren't, and it dates the game to the beer-hall it came from.

Chips

A stack that gets shorter, with the lost chips sliding off toward the pot. Best physical metaphor and the most expensive to draw; overlapping discs also count badly at small sizes.

The pick: candles on the round table (plate 01), where there's room and the motion earns its keep; pips everywhere they're incidental. Whichever wins, put the number next to it — a screen reader currently gets six list items, and a red-green colourblind player gets a row of similar dots. 4 lives beside the glyph fixes both and costs nothing.

Build cost Client-only, all four. The candle flame is one CSS gradient and the smoke is a keyframe; skip both under prefers-reduced-motion.

Five rows that all say "0/8 players · waiting" is a database query wearing a list.

Draw the tables as tables. Filled seats are brass dots on the rim, empty ones are outlines — so "nearly ready" and "just opened" look different from across the room, and you join the one that's about to start instead of reading five identical lines.

A table mid-game keeps its ring lit and offers Watch instead of Join, which is already the behaviour and currently reads as a subtly different button label. New table is the empty chair in the corner.

Whimsy budget, well spent: the dots on a waiting table drift very slightly, like people shifting in their seats. Idle tables feel occupied rather than abandoned.

Build cost Client-only — the lobby's TableSummary already carries name, seat count and phase. Showing which seats are filled is cosmetic; any consistent arrangement reads fine.

The game ends with a trophy and a button back to the lobby. It should end with a story you want to send someone.

The last round as a filmstrip — every claim in order, the doubt in red, the truth at the end — is the shareable object. It's six small cells and it explains exactly how the game was won, including to the two people who were already out and watching.

Per-player stats turn a one-off table into a thing people compare. "You told the truth twice all game" is the kind of line that gets screenshotted, and it falls straight out of data the room already has in its log.

Rematch is the missing button. A table is single-use by design, so the honest version creates a new table pre-seeded with the same players and drops the link into everyone's open socket before they close the tab.

Build cost The real one on this page. Stats need per-game tallies; the filmstrip needs the round's claims kept past the reveal; rematch needs a new table created from an old one and a way to tell the old room's sockets about it. The finished-game row in D1 is the natural home for the summary.

Your cup

The page is phone-first and should stay that way, but above about 900px it currently just centres a 34rem column and leaves two-thirds of the screen as felt-coloured wallpaper. Three columns costs nothing and makes the laptop player feel like they're at the same table: roster left, felt centre, log right, with your own cup tucked bottom-right where a real player's hand would be.

The greedy version of this idea: a table view URL you open on a laptop or a TV in the middle of the room, showing the felt, everyone's claims and the reveal at full size while the phones keep only the buttons. That is a party game, and it's the one thing on this page that would change how Mia gets played rather than how it looks.

Build cost Three columns: client-only, a media query and a grid. The shared table view needs a spectator socket that never gets a seat and a route that renders it — the watch path already proves the server can do this.

12

Small delights

cheap, mostly
  • Dice that landA 400ms tumble with a settle-bounce, and the value doesn't read until they stop. Rolling should take a beat; instantaneous dice feel like a number generator, which is exactly what they are and exactly what you want to hide.
  • Haptics on the turnOne short tap when the cup reaches you, a double when someone doubts you, and a long buzz when you lose a life. Phones in pockets on a pub table need this more than they need the countdown.
  • The last ten secondsThe felt itself desaturates toward red as the clock runs down, so you feel the timer in your peripheral vision without watching a number.
  • Name your own poisonAuto-names like Slightly Wet are one of the best things about the product. Lean in: let a player reroll the name as many times as they like before the game starts, and show the dice-roll animation when they do.
  • Sound, optional and tinyThree samples: a cup rattle, a die on wood, a low thud for the reveal. Default off with a speaker toggle in the top bar, because half of these games start in a room where everyone's phone is on.
  • “They're thinking”An ellipsis under the active player's name while their turn runs. It costs one broadcast, it makes remote play feel inhabited, and it makes a stalled phone visibly different from a long think.
  • The empty-table screenSitting alone waiting for friends currently looks like an error state. Give it something to do: a practice cup you can roll for fun while you wait, and the join link big enough to read out loud over a phone call.
  • Honest-player badgeIf you win a game having never once bluffed, say so, loudly, on the result card. It will happen roughly never, which is the point.
  • Timer expiry, in characterWhen the server auto-plays for an idle phone, don't say "timed out". Say “Mild Peril says nothing, so the cup decides for them.” Same event, and it keeps the fiction intact while they're getting a drink.
  • Double-Mia thunderThe one rule in the game with a doubled penalty deserves one piece of special-case theatre. Two lives should not disappear the same way one does.

If it were three things

First

02 · The ladder

Biggest gain per line of code on the page. It's client-only, it uses ranking data that already exists, and it turns the game's hardest rule into something you learn by looking. Nothing else here is that cheap and that important.

Then

04 · The showdown

The reveal is the emotional payload of every round and it currently reads like a log line. Staging it properly is a day of CSS and it's what people will remember about playing.

Then

01 · Table in the round

The one structural change. Riskier on eight seats and worth prototyping against a full table before committing — but it's what makes the thing feel like a game rather than a form.

Everything in Act Two needs server state the room doesn't keep yet. That's not an argument against it — it's an argument for doing Act One first and seeing whether the game still wants it afterwards.

Mockups are live HTML and CSS, drawn at roughly 0.77× phone scale. Nothing here is wired to the real client.
